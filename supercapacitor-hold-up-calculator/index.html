Supercapacitor hold-up

Estimate how long a capacitor bank can support a constant-power load.

Example: 10 F from 5 V down to 3 V stores 80 J in the usable voltage window. At 80% efficiency and a 4 W load, estimated hold-up is 16 seconds.

How it works & model limitations

Stored energy = ½ × C × (start voltage² − minimum voltage²). Hold-up time = stored energy × efficiency ÷ load power.

Enter effective bank capacitance, not individual cell capacitance for a series bank. Efficiency is treated as constant. ESR voltage drop, converter current limits, startup, leakage, balancing losses, aging and temperature are not modeled. Set minimum voltage to reflect the actual converter and load requirements. What size supercapacitor do I need for a target hold-up time?

Use the start voltage, cutoff voltage, efficiency and load from the calculator above. Change the target below to estimate the minimum effective bank capacitance.

For 4 W over 20 seconds, the load needs 80 J. At 80% efficiency the bank must supply 100 J. Discharging from 5 V to 3 V yields 8 J per farad, so the ideal minimum is 12.5 F. Two identical series cells would each need at least 25 F before allowances.

C = 2 × load watts × target seconds / [efficiency fraction × (start voltage² − cutoff voltage²)]. Use effective capacitance at operating conditions. ESR, aging and converter limits can shorten usable time. Check cell-to-bank conversion or see the hold-up planning pack.

Will a 10 F supercapacitor support a 4 W load for 20 seconds?

With a start voltage of 5 V and a minimum of 3 V, the voltage window contains 80 J. At 80% conversion efficiency, 64 J reaches the load. That gives 16 seconds at 4 W, so this example does not meet a 20-second target.

Common questions

Why use effective bank capacitance?

Series and parallel connections change the bank capacitance. Enter the effective value for the complete bank, not the capacitance printed on a single cell. Check cell voltage ratings separately.

Does the estimated time include ESR?

No. Resistive voltage drop, changing efficiency, tolerances and leakage can shorten practical hold-up. Verify the minimum converter input voltage during a measured discharge at the actual load.

Which supercap discharge model does this use?

This calculator estimates hold-up time for a constant-power load through a converter with assumed constant efficiency: t = efficiency × C × (Vstart² − Vcutoff²) / (2 × load watts). It is not a resistor-load RC time-constant calculator or a constant-current discharge model.

Use the effective bank capacitance for series-connected cells. Check converter cutoff, cell balancing, ESR voltage drop, leakage and startup current separately; the energy estimate alone does not prove a usable backup time.

Enough stored energy, but is capacitor ripple loss acceptable?

The hold-up calculator answers an energy question. It does not estimate ripple heating. Review RMS current and the capacitor's ESR at the relevant frequencies separately before treating an energy result as a complete design check.

Illustrative loss check: 2 A RMS at 0.040 ohm ESR contributes 0.160 W; a separate orthogonal frequency component of 0.5 A RMS at 0.120 ohm contributes 0.030 W. Their total is 0.190 W. These are assumed values, not measured component data. Do not add duplicate or non-orthogonal components this way.

Loss alone does not determine temperature or lifetime. A temperature estimate needs a mounting-specific thermal model; capacitor voltage, ripple-current and temperature ratings still need separate manufacturer review. Manufacturer explanation of RMS current, ESR and loss.

Supercap discharge time: choose the load model first

The calculator above uses constant output power and a fixed converter efficiency. A constant-current load or a resistor connected directly to the capacitor follows a different voltage curve. Choose the model that matches your circuit before comparing times.

Constant power: a regulated converter

t = η C (Vstart² − Vcutoff²) / (2 P). With effective bank capacitance C = 10 F, 5 V down to 3 V, output power P = 4 W and efficiency η = 0.80, usable output energy is 64 J and estimated time is 16 seconds. Capacitor current increases as voltage falls: approximately 1 A at 5 V and 1.67 A at 3 V.

Constant current: a direct 1 A load

t = C (Vstart − Vcutoff) / I. The same 10 F bank from 5 V to 3 V at a constant 1 A gives 20 seconds. Voltage falls linearly in this ideal model. Load power falls from 5 W to 3 W; this is not the same demand as the 4 W converter example.

Resistor: a direct 5 ohm load

t = R C ln(Vstart / Vcutoff). For 5 ohms and 10 F from 5 V to 3 V, t = 50 × ln(5/3) = 25.54 seconds. Current falls from 1 A to 0.6 A and power from 5 W to 1.8 W. RC = 50 seconds is a time constant, not the time to this 3 V cutoff.

These examples compare assumptions, not competing capacitor performance. Use seconds, farads, volts, watts, amperes and ohms in the equations. Require C > 0, Vstart > Vcutoff > 0 and a positive load. Converter efficiency belongs only in the converter model; it is not a multiplier for the direct-load examples.

Before relying on a predicted time

  • Use effective bank capacitance, including series connection and tolerance.
  • Check ESR-induced voltage drop and the actual load or converter cutoff.
  • Check cell voltage limits, balancing, leakage, temperature and aging.
  • Measure the voltage curve with the real load: constant capacitance and fixed efficiency are approximations, not a guaranteed backup time.
