How it works & model limitations

Daily GB = cameras × Mbps × 0.45 × recording hours. Required TB = daily GB × retention days × (1 + overhead / 100) ÷ 1000. GB and TB use decimal units.

Enter the measured encoded bitrate, including audio if recorded. AI processing does not determine bitrate. Variable bitrate, motion-triggered recording, metadata, filesystem capacity and RAID reduce predictability. Overhead is an added allowance, not a percentage of disk space reserved. Aggregate bitrate assumes all cameras record simultaneously; network protocol overhead is excluded.

How much storage do four cameras need for seven days?

Four cameras at 4 Mbps each generate 172.8 decimal GB per day when recording continuously. Seven days gives 1.2096 TB before overhead. Adding a 20% allowance brings the planning value to 1.45152 TB.

Common questions

Can I use this for CCTV, an NVR or a DVR?

Yes, when you know the recorded encoded bitrate per camera. Enter that measured Mbps value, camera count, daily recording hours and retention days. The calculation estimates video data volume; it does not select a recorder or certify disk capacity, redundancy or write performance.

Can resolution alone predict storage?

No. Codec settings, frame rate, scene detail and compression affect encoded bitrate. Measure representative footage and enter Mbps per camera. This is not a resolution-to-bitrate estimator.

Are TB and TiB interchangeable?

No. This calculator uses decimal units: 1 TB = 1,000 GB. Software may display binary TiB instead. Filesystem use, redundancy and reserve space also reduce available recording capacity.

Check the assumptions behind your calculation

Use these free worked guides to choose better inputs and avoid common interpretation mistakes.

  • CCTV storage: 20% overhead vs 20% free disk space

    Adding 20% to video storage and reserving 20% of a disk are different calculations. Compare both approaches before planning NVR retention capacity.

  • Estimate camera bitrate from recorded file growth

    A resolution label is not a storage measurement. Recorded file growth over a known time interval gives a useful starting point for the bitrate input, provided you know what those bytes contain.

  • Combine cameras with different bitrates and recording schedules

    A single camera-count input assumes a common bitrate and recording schedule. For a mixed installation, calculate each group separately and add its data volume before applying a shared allowance.

  • Compare decimal TB with binary TiB without losing video capacity

    A recorder can display a smaller-looking capacity number without any bytes being missing. Decimal terabytes and binary tebibytes use different divisors, and they should not be mixed in a retention calculation.
