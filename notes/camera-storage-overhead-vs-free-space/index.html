CCTV storage: 20% overhead vs 20% free disk space

Adding 20% to video storage and reserving 20% of a disk are different calculations. Compare both approaches before planning NVR retention capacity.

All tools and notes

Start with the recorded video volume

For four cameras at 4 Mbps each, recording continuously for seven days, the encoded-video estimate is 1.2096 decimal TB. This comes from 4 cameras × 4,000,000 bits/s × 604,800 seconds ÷ 8 bits/byte ÷ 1,000,000,000,000 bytes/TB. Use measured representative bitrate rather than resolution alone. This is an illustrative constant-bitrate estimate, not measured recorder performance.

An added allowance multiplies the requirement

Adding 20% means 1.2096 × 1.20 = 1.45152 TB. The extra 0.24192 TB is 20% of the original video amount. If that extra amount remained empty, it would be only 16.6667% of the resulting capacity, because 0.24192 ÷ 1.45152 = 1/6. Our free camera calculator uses this added-allowance meaning for its Storage overhead field. It does not implement a free-space reservation.

A free-space target divides by the occupied fraction

To keep 20% of the usable disk capacity empty, video may occupy at most 80%. Required usable capacity is therefore 1.2096 ÷ 0.80 = 1.512 TB. The planned empty portion is 0.3024 TB. This is 0.06048 TB, or 60.48 decimal GB, more than adding 20%. For a reserve fraction r, use required capacity = stored data ÷ (1 − r), with 0 ≤ r < 1. A 100% reserve leaves no capacity for recording.

Apply separate requirements in the correct order

If you genuinely need both a 20% data allowance and a 20% free-space reserve, apply them separately: 1.2096 × 1.20 ÷ 0.80 = 1.8144 TB of usable capacity. Do not add the two percentages and call it 40%. Avoid counting the same allowance twice: document whether metadata, audio and other recorder data are included in your measured bitrate or covered separately. A hypothetical usable 2 TB volume with a 20% reserve allows 1.6 TB of stored data; compare against this amount, not its full capacity.

Before choosing retention or a disk

Record camera count, recording hours, measured average bitrate, retention duration, the reason for every allowance, and usable capacity after formatting and redundancy. Confirm whether the recorder treats its limit as a quota, a free-space threshold, or an overwrite policy. Decimal TB and binary TiB must be converted to the same unit. Variable bitrate and scene changes can alter the real volume; Axis discusses how bitrate-control modes affect storage predictability. Neither percentage in this example is a universal vendor recommendation. Check your recorder documentation and verify actual recording growth before relying on the plan.

Check the assumptions behind your calculation

Use these free worked guides to choose better inputs and avoid common interpretation mistakes.

  • Estimate camera bitrate from recorded file growth

    A resolution label is not a storage measurement. Recorded file growth over a known time interval gives a useful starting point for the bitrate input, provided you know what those bytes contain.

  • Combine cameras with different bitrates and recording schedules

    A single camera-count input assumes a common bitrate and recording schedule. For a mixed installation, calculate each group separately and add its data volume before applying a shared allowance.

  • Compare decimal TB with binary TiB without losing video capacity

    A recorder can display a smaller-looking capacity number without any bytes being missing. Decimal terabytes and binary tebibytes use different divisors, and they should not be mixed in a retention calculation.
