Why 4.7 kohm is not a universal answer

At 3.3 V, 0.4 V VOL, 3 mA sink capability, 200 pF and a 300 ns rise limit, effective resistance must be between 0.966667 and 1.770329 kohm. With ±5% tolerance, one nominal resistor must lie between 1.017544 and 1.686028 kohm.

A 4.7 kohm part reaches 4.935 kohm at its high corner, giving about 836.285 ns rise time. A 1.5 kohm part reaches 1.575 kohm and gives 266.900 ns; its low corner requires 2.03509 mA at the entered VOL. Only the second choice meets both entered bounds in this model.

What if four boards each have a pullup?

Four identical 4.7 kohm pullups on the same line give 1.175 kohm nominal effective resistance. At ±5%, the range is 1.11625–1.23375 kohm. The model predicts a worst rise time of 209.071 ns and 2.59798 mA at the low-voltage boundary. More parallel resistors improve rise time but increase sink demand.

What if no resistor fits?

At 600 pF and the same 300 ns limit, the maximum effective resistance is 0.590110 kohm, below the 0.966667 kohm minimum. No passive resistor satisfies those entered limits. Check wiring capacitance, topology and permitted timing; do not simply choose the midpoint. This example is a constraint conflict, not approval for a particular I2C mode's capacitance limit.

Equations and boundaries

Rmin = (Vpullup − VOL) / IOL. Rmax = tr / (0.8473 × Cbus). The rise estimate is the single-RC 30–70% charging interval. For n equal pullups, Req = R/n. Low and high resistance corners multiply Req by (1 − tolerance) and (1 + tolerance).

The displayed nominal window applies to each of the n identical resistors. The current result is evaluated at the entered VOL boundary; it is not the maximum possible low-state current. At a lower actual VOL the current can be higher.

1000 ns, 300 ns and 120 ns are common Standard-, Fast- and Fast-mode Plus rise-time limits. Enter the applicable device and system requirement rather than assuming clock frequency alone determines compliance. This tool does not verify mode capacitance limits, fall time, setup/hold timing or device compatibility.

Mixed resistor values, level shifters, active accelerators, leakage, distributed cables and non-RC waveforms need separate analysis. Supply and capacitance tolerances are not automatically added: enter defensible upper bounds. Verify traces on hardware, including probe capacitance.

Reference: Texas Instruments SLVA689A: I2C Bus Pullup Resistor Calculation. Equations checked 23 October 2026. This independent tool is not endorsed by TI.

My scope shows a slow I2C edge. What should I check?

Start by checking the measurement thresholds. The calculator uses 30–70% of the pullup rail. A scope set to 10–90% reports a longer interval, so comparing that number directly with a 30–70% limit can create a false failure.

For an ideal single-pole RC edge, the 10–90% interval is ln(9) × RC and the 30–70% interval is ln(7/3) × RC. Their ratio is about 2.5932. An illustrative 600 ns reading at 10–90% corresponds to about 231.37 ns at 30–70%. This conversion is not valid for an arbitrary waveform: change the scope thresholds and remeasure if there is ringing, active acceleration or a multi-stage edge.

  1. Rise too slow: inventory fitted pullups, cable and device capacitance. Include probe loading. A smaller resistance may help rise time but increases sink demand.
  2. VOL too high: check the transmitting device, all parallel pullups and the device-specific sink guarantee. Do not assume every breakout board has its pullup removed.
  3. Both electrical checks pass but communication fails: inspect addresses, setup/hold timing, stretching, power sequencing and translator behavior. Passing these two bounds is not a complete protocol test.

Keep a review record

  1. Inventory all fitted pullups on one line, including breakout boards.
  2. Record capacitance assumptions and device-specific VOL/IOL limits.
  3. Export the calculation and measure the 30–70% rise time on the assembled bus.
  4. Retest after adding cables, boards or measurement probes.
